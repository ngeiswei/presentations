* Outline

- My talk is in two parts

  1. How to estimate the probability of a conjecture to be a theorem,
  specifically using PLN, a logic that can deal with uncertainty.

  2. How to use these estimates to guide reasoning.

* State of the Art

- Regarding the problem of estimating the probability of a conjecture

* PLN Recall:

- So now I'm going to have to give you a flash course on PLN.  In 2
  minutes.

- PLN stands for Probabilistic Logic Networks, was invented by Ben
  Goertzel, Matt Ikle and few others.  It is highly inspired by NAL,
  which stands for Non-Axiomatic Logic (even though the NAL was
  published after the PLN book, it was actually invented a little
  before).

- I want to mention Subjective Logic as well that has been developped
  in parallel, it is not PLN but it has some things in common with it,
  notably the use of second order probability distribution.  I am
  going to explain.

* Trad vs PLN:

- OK, so how PLN works.  I like to put it this way.  In a traditional
  logical system, you only go from theory to theorems.  You define a
  theory, and then you derive the theorems in that theory.  In PLN
  however, you can actually go both ways.  You can start from a theory
  and derive theorems, but you can also start from theorems and derive
  what theories would derive these theorems.  A good example would be
  physics, for instance theorems could be observations of the movement
  of bodies and a theory could be General Relativity.  PLN, in theory
  anyway, enables you to do that.

- But so how?  And the idea is to introduce inference rules that go in
  reverse.  So if we have a rule that goes from Theory to theorem,
  we're going to introduce one or more rules that go the other way.

* PLN reverse:

- Here's an example: Deduction, if A imples B and B imples C then A
  implies C.  How do we take the reverse.  We introduce two rules.
  Induction: that starts from the conclusion, so we move the
  conclusion as the first premise, then we keep the second premise as
  it was, and we infer the first premise.  Then we do the same for the
  untouched premise, this rule is called abduction.  I will explain
  why this one is called induction and why this one is called
  abduction.

- So, I here you coming saying "but wait, this is not correct" only
  deduction is strictly correct, induction and abduction are not.  And
  yes you would be right, in a traditional binary logic, but PLN is
  not a binary logic.

* PLN Truth Value:

- in PLN, a truth value, is not binary, but it is not even a
  probability, it is a second order probability.  Meaning, a
  probability of a probability.

- What can you describe with this: you can describe complete
  uncertainty of a proposition, which would be represented by a flat
  uniform distribution.  That is, you not only do not know if the
  proposition is true or false, you don't even know the probability of
  being true of false, it could be any probability.

- The simplest way to describe such second order distribution is with
  what we call a simple truth value, characterized by two numbers: a
  strength that is a proxy for a probability and a confidence,
  indicating how much confidence you have in that strength.

- So here are some examples of second order distribution, as you
  accumulate evidence.  1, 2, etc, You get away from uniformity and
  shift to a side.  The more evidence you gather the thiner the second
  order distribution becomes.  That would be if you have negative
  evidence.  And of course you can have a combination of positive and
  negative evidence.
